### Question

What is accelerated slipstream, and why does it matter in a twin?

### Answer

- Each propeller blows high-velocity air over the section of wing directly behind it, so that part of the wing produces substantially more lift than the rest.
- With both engines running, the effect is symmetric and you never notice it.
- When one engine quits, the lift behind the dead engine drops away while the operating side keeps its accelerated slipstream. The result is a **rolling moment toward the dead engine**, on top of the yaw from asymmetric thrust.
- It's also one of the four factors that make one engine critical, and P-factor is what links the two. At a positive angle of attack the **descending blade** produces more thrust, so it accelerates more air over the wing behind it. On two clockwise-turning propellers the descending blade of each is on its **right** side, which displaces the center of the added lift to the right of each engine's centerline.
- That displacement is **outboard on the right engine and inboard on the left**. So losing the left engine leaves the right engine producing its extra lift at a **longer arm** from the airplane's longitudinal axis, and the rolling moment toward the dead engine is stronger. Losing the right engine leaves the left engine working at a shorter arm, and the roll is weaker - which is one of the four reasons the **left engine is critical**.
