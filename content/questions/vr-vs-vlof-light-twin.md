### Question

What rotation and liftoff speeds should you use in a light twin, and why not rotate as early as possible?

### Answer

- Use the AFM's published rotation and lift-off speeds. Where the AFM doesn't publish them, the AFH calls for a minimum of **V<sub>MC</sub> plus 5 knots** as V<sub>R</sub> - a margin above the red line, not the red line itself.
- After lift-off, with both engines running, the AFH has you accelerate to **V<sub>Y</sub>**, the all-engine best rate of climb speed, and hold it to a safe single-engine maneuvering altitude. **V<sub>YSE</sub>** is the gate for the gear - the AFH's general recommendation is gear up no later than V<sub>YSE</sub> - and it's the speed you go to if an engine actually quits, not the normal two-engine climb speed.
- Rotating early puts you airborne at or below V<sub>MC</sub>. If an engine fails there, you have no ability to maintain directional control, and the airplane will roll toward the dead engine with the ground very close.
- Accelerating on the runway costs a little distance but means that by the time you are airborne, an engine failure is a controllable event.

This is the practical reason many light twin takeoff briefings are built around a hard rule: still on the runway below that speed means abort.
