### Question

How does cabin pressurization work, and what happens if the pressurization source fails?

### Answer

- A compressor feeds conditioned air into the cabin faster than it can leak out. An **outflow valve** meters how fast it escapes, and that balance sets cabin altitude.
- The air source depends on the powerplant: **bleed air** from a turbine or turboprop engine, bleed air from a **turbocharger** on a piston airplane, or an engine-driven **supercharger or compressor** on some installations.
- Key terms:
  - **Cabin altitude** - The pressure altitude the occupants actually feel.
  - **Differential pressure** - The difference between cabin and ambient pressure, limited by the structure. Max differential is what sets the highest cabin altitude you can hold at a given flight level.
  - **Cabin rate of climb** - How fast cabin altitude is changing, controlled for comfort.
- Safety devices: a **pressure relief valve** (caps maximum differential), a **negative pressure relief valve** (prevents ambient pressure from exceeding cabin pressure), and a **dump valve** to depressurize deliberately.

#### When the pressurization source is lost or degraded

- The cabin climbs toward ambient altitude. How fast depends on whether it is a gradual loss of source air or a structural failure.
- The response is the same regardless of airplane: **oxygen on, then descend** to a safe altitude. [§ 91.211](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-C/section-91.211) oxygen requirements come back into play the moment the cabin can no longer be held low.
- A **rapid decompression** adds hazards beyond hypoxia - fogging, noise, flying debris, cold, and a much shorter time of useful consciousness than the cabin altitude alone suggests.

**In a twin specifically:** losing an engine may cost you **half your source of bleed air**, so the system may be unable to hold the differential even though the airplane is still flying. Expect the cabin to climb. That's a second reason a high-altitude engine failure means descending.

**In a pressurized single** (typically a turboprop, and a few piston singles), there is no second source at all - a failure of that engine or its bleed system means the cabin will climb, which is why these airplanes carry oxygen and why the emergency descent is a memory item.
