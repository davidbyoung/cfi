### Question

How does cabin pressurization work, and what happens if the pressurization source fails?

### Answer

- A compressor feeds air into the cabin faster than it leaks out, and an **outflow valve** meters the escape. That balance sets **cabin altitude** - the pressure altitude the occupants actually feel.
- The source depends on the powerplant: **bleed air** from a turbine or turboprop, bleed air from a **turbocharger** on a piston airplane, or an engine-driven supercharger.
- **Differential pressure** is the cabin-to-ambient difference, limited by the structure. Max differential is what sets the lowest cabin altitude you can hold at a given flight level. **Cabin rate of climb** is how fast that cabin altitude is moving, controlled for comfort.
- Safety devices: a **pressure relief valve** (caps maximum differential), a **negative pressure relief valve** (keeps ambient from exceeding cabin pressure), and a **dump valve** to depressurize deliberately.

**Losing the pressurization source.** The cabin climbs toward ambient - gradually if it's a loss of source air, explosively if it's structural. The shape of the response is **oxygen on, then descend**, though the AFM procedure governs and a smoke or fire event can reorder the priorities. [§ 91.211](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-C/section-91.211) comes back into play the moment the cabin can no longer be held low. A **rapid decompression** adds fogging, noise, debris, cold, and a much shorter time of useful consciousness than the cabin altitude alone suggests.

**In a twin**, losing an engine may cost **half your bleed air**, so the system may not hold the differential even though the airplane flies fine - another reason a high-altitude engine failure means descending. **In a pressurized single** there is no second source at all, which is why those airplanes carry oxygen and treat the emergency descent as a memory item.
