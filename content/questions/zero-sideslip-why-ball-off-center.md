### Question

Why is the ball off center when you're flying zero sideslip?

### Answer

#### Why bank and rudder together produce zero sideslip

- Left engine out: the right engine's thrust yaws the nose toward the dead engine. **Right rudder** stops the yaw.
- But the rudder is a lifting surface. The side force it produces on the tail acts **toward the dead engine**, and with the wings level nothing opposes it - so the airplane slips through the air, fuselage skewed to the relative wind.
- **Bank ~2° toward the operating engine.** The lift vector tilts, and its **horizontal component** acts toward the good engine, opposing the rudder's side force.
- With the two in balance, the airplane no longer has to slip to make up the difference. The fuselage aligns with the relative wind: **zero sideslip.**

#### Why that leaves the ball off center

- The horizontal component of lift is what normally turns an airplane. Here it is fully committed to opposing the rudder, so the net sideways force is **zero** - the flight path stays straight and the heading holds.
- In a normal **coordinated turn** the ball centers because the bank produces a turn, and the turn's inertia pushes the ball outboard by exactly as much as gravity pulls it toward the low wing.
- In zero sideslip there is **no turn**, so nothing offsets gravity. The ball responds to gravity alone and settles toward the low wing - **one-third to one-half a ball width toward the operating engine.** Its displacement is really just reading your **bank angle**.

#### Why this contradicts the single

- In a single, a **slip** (ball toward the low wing) and a **skid** (ball toward the high wing) are the same thing in opposite directions - both are sideslip, and both show on the ball.
- What a single doesn't have is a **standing** sideways force. The rudder can certainly make one - a forward slip is exactly that - but it is a force you are choosing to hold, and releasing it removes the force and the sideslip together. Centering the ball and zeroing the sideslip normally coincide, which is why **"step on the ball"** works in either direction.
- With an engine out, the rudder you hold is opposing a yawing moment from **thrust**, not from the relative wind. It stays there whether or not the fuselage is aligned, and the ball - which only sees the sum of the forces - cannot tell the two sources apart.
- So "balanced" now has two solutions: let the **air** balance the rudder, which takes a sideslip, or let the **horizontal component of lift** balance it, which does not. Only the second is zero sideslip.

In a single, a centered ball means no sideslip. In a twin with an engine inoperative, it means **no bank** - and with rudder held in, no bank means you are slipping. Chasing the ball back to center from a correct 2° of bank takes still more rudder, restores the sideslip, and turns you instead of holding heading.
