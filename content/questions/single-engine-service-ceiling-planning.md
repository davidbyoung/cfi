### Question

How does the single-engine service ceiling change how you plan a flight?

### Answer

- It tells you the altitude the airplane can actually sustain with one engine feathered. If you are cruising **above** it, an engine failure means a **drift down** to that altitude, not level flight.
- Over terrain, that turns into a specific question: is the single-engine service ceiling above the **highest terrain and obstacles** along your route, with margin?
- If it isn't, you need a different route, a lower weight, a cooler time of day, or an honest acceptance that an engine failure means an off-airport landing.
- It also shapes your escape plan - knowing which direction leads to lower terrain matters far more in a twin that can only descend slowly than in one that can climb.

This is the planning item that separates flying a twin from simply having two engines.
