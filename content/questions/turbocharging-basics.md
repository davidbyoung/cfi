### Question

How does turbocharging work, and what do you need to know to operate it?

### Answer

- A turbocharger uses **exhaust gas** to spin a turbine driving a compressor, which raises induction air pressure so the engine can make sea-level manifold pressure well above sea level.
- A **wastegate** controls how much exhaust bypasses the turbine. An **automatic** one holds target manifold pressure for you; a **fixed** or **manual** one puts overboost protection on the pilot.
- **Critical altitude** is the highest altitude at which the system can still maintain rated manifold pressure. Above it the wastegate is fully closed and power falls off like a normally aspirated engine.
- **Turbonormalized** systems only restore roughly sea-level manifold pressure (~29-30 inHg). A true **turbocharged** installation may be **ground boosted** above sea-level values, which makes overboost limits and cylinder pressures a bigger deal.

**Operating concerns**

- **Overboost** - move the throttle smoothly, especially with a fixed or manual wastegate.
- **Heat** - turbine inlet and exhaust temperatures run high. Watch TIT/EGT and CHT, and observe any **cooldown** at idle before shutdown so hot oil isn't left sitting in the bearings with no flow.
- **Shock cooling** - plan descents early rather than chopping power.
- **Oil** - the turbo is lubricated by engine oil, so an oil problem is now a turbocharger problem too.
- **The airplane still has limits** - a turbo extends _engine_ performance, not the airframe's ceiling or the wing. Induction icing, [§ 91.211](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-C/section-91.211) oxygen requirements, and your own hypoxia tolerance all arrive before the engine runs out of manifold pressure.

**In a single**, the payoff is climb rate at altitude, higher cruise TAS, and topping weather or terrain - and the trap is being at oxygen altitudes in an unpressurized airplane.

**In a twin**, you get a much better single-engine ceiling and climb, but **V<sub>MC</sub> stays high**: a normally aspirated twin's V<sub>MC</sub> falls with altitude as the operating engine loses power, while a turbocharged twin holds full asymmetric thrust to critical altitude, so V<sub>MC</sub> can remain **above** stall speed much higher up.

Specific numbers, wastegate type, and cooldown procedure are all AFM-dependent.
